Before you talk

How the room goes

Cue. The summary comes first. Do not summarize the paper again. About three minutes for the opening and these four questions. Groups of about four, one question each. Ask for two answers that do not match, and the part of the paper each answer is using. Ask someone who has not spoken before going back to the same people. If a comment leaves the paper, ask whether you are talking about the product or about what people still have.

Say this · about 1 min

Opening

The authors argue that participatory design is not the activity itself. A workshop can look the same in participatory design and in co-design. What they think is different is why you run it, and what people still have when you leave. They call that reason a mechanism: mutual learning, who gets a say, and a group that can keep the work going. They lay the full set of activities out as an ideal, and they say almost no real project does every piece. I am not going to retell the paper. I want us to argue about what still counts.

Question 1

Future Workshop

A Future Workshop runs in three steps: people name what is broken, they imagine a future with the limits turned off, then they make a plan that could actually happen. The authors say that shared vision only works if the designers cannot already see the ending.

If we walk in with the design already picked, what is left for the people in the room to decide?

Cue. If the room agrees too fast, ask whether people still learned something when the ending was already picked, and whether that learning is enough to count.

Question 2

Field study

They say what you learn in a field study is for the people who live that work, and the designers learn it with them.

If those people never see the writeup, what did they get out of being studied?

Cue. If the room says they can just read the report later, ask whether the paper wants them deciding what the study means, not only reading it afterward. Then ask a second group what those people walked away with.

Question 3

After the researchers leave

In fablab@school.dk the team spent time on a community of teachers, and on talking with people from pupils up to politicians, so the work would still be there after the researchers left.

If a project ends and the only thing left is the technology, what did it fail to build?

Cue. Ask one group to defend the technology as the result, and another to defend the community of teachers as the result. Get both answers in the room before you pick.

Question 4

What counts as success

They call this whole picture an ideal, and they say almost no real project does every piece. Hertzum and Simonsen score a project by whether it did what the stakeholders asked for at the start. They look at effects in real use.

When the prototype works and the people in the room cannot name anything they gained, what should we count as success?

Cue. If the room slides into usability, ask what Hertzum and Simonsen actually measured. Then ask whether a working prototype with no personal gain is a failed project, or an incomplete one, since the authors say almost nobody hits the full ideal.

Wrap-up · about 2 min

Stop

The last two minutes are for one disagreement we did not settle.
