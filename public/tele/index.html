Slide 1 · Title slide · 15 sec

Workload Aware Calendar

Title and team introduction.

Howdy. We’re Group 0, and this is our Phase Five design for the Workload-Aware Calendar, or WAC. We’re building a Chrome extension to help students see the work behind their deadlines and plan when to do it.

Slide 2 · 25 sec

Introduction to WAC

Follow Canvas → WAC → Calendar, then the student’s adjustment arrow.

A calendar tells us when an assignment is due, but we still have to figure out how much work it needs. In this diagram, assignment information goes into WAC, and WAC adds deadlines and workload to the calendar. The student can then adjust the estimate. That feedback matters because the same assignment can take different people different amounts of time.

Slide 3 · 25 sec

Design Goals & Requirements

Three cards: integration, ease of use, visual clarity. The deck skips slides 4–8 next.

We have three requirements. It should fit into Google Calendar and load assignment information without making students type everything again. Estimates should be easy to change, and students should control their reminders. Finally, workload should be readable alongside the course colors. We’re using low, medium, and high effort labels, with plus signs and optional patterns.

Slide 9 · 30 sec

Core Chrome Extension Specifications

Four sections: data, visual cues, reminders, work blocks.

For the prototype, we can use sample assignments with the same fields as Canvas: title, due date, points, and description. Each gets an initial effort estimate that the student can override. The calendar adds workload cues while keeping course colors. Students choose how many reminders they want and schedule smaller work blocks before the deadline. Those are the main pieces we want to connect.

Slide 10 · 25 sec

Cognitive & Estimation Rationale

Explain the design choices. No need to read every citation or the numerical example.

The reason for putting this together is to make weekly planning easier. Students can see their workload across courses instead of keeping track of it all in their heads. Using a familiar calendar should also make the transition easier. The estimate is a starting point, so correcting it needs to be quick. We still need to test whether these choices actually make planning easier.

Slide 11 · 25 sec

Accessibility & Social Issues

Text and patterns on the left; workload and stress on the right.

For accessibility, effort needs to be understandable without distinguishing colors. That’s why we pair course colors with text and optional patterns. We also want students to keep time open for things outside class. Adjustable reminders and earlier work blocks are meant to support that. Reducing stress is a goal of the design, and we shouldn’t treat it as a result we’ve already demonstrated.

Slide 12 · 30 sec

Testing the Chrome Extension

Study comparison → measures → Phase 6. Dates shown: November 19 and December 3–4.

Our next step is to compare planning with WAC turned off and on. The same students try both versions using sample assignments. We’ll look at how difficult planning feels, whether they schedule work earlier, how they use reminders, and whether the workload cues are clear. Phase Six is the implementation checkpoint on November nineteenth, followed by the final presentation and demo in early December.

Slide 14 · 40 sec · after Q&A in the deck

Preliminary Design: plan and review

Walk left to right: assignment list → split work → review and publish.

Here’s what that flow looks like. On the left, students can see estimated effort, time already planned, and assignments that still need time. In the middle, this literature review has a three-hour estimate. Choosing sixty-minute blocks splits it into three sessions around the calendar’s existing events. On the right, the student reviews those blocks. Saving keeps the plan local until they choose Publish plan.

Slide 15 · 20 sec · after Q&A in the deck

Preliminary Design: Google Calendar

Point to the three literature-review blocks in Thursday’s column.

After publishing, those three work blocks appear in Google Calendar. You can see them here on Thursday, between the existing events. The assignment now has specific time set aside before it’s due. This is the connection we’re trying to make between seeing the workload and deciding when to work on it.

Slide 16 · 20 sec · after Q&A in the deck

Other Potential Designs: pattern and shape

Top: one, two, or three stripes. Bottom: one, two, or three notches.

We also explored different ways to show effort. The top version uses stripes inside the event. The bottom changes the edge with notches. Both keep the event’s color. We’d want to compare how easily students understand these cues, especially when the calendar is crowded. These are alternatives we’re considering.

Slide 17 · 20 sec · after Q&A in the deck

Other Potential Designs: clock and pointed shapes

Top: partially filled clock icons. Bottom: rounded and pointed event shapes.

These are two more alternatives. The top uses small clock-like icons with different amounts filled in. The bottom changes the event shape. Both try to make workload visible at a glance. We still need to check whether people understand the meaning without having to stop and decode each symbol.

Slide 5 · Currently skipped

Core Design Thesis

Large statement about effort beside deadlines.

Our main idea is to put estimated effort beside deadlines so students can plan their week earlier. Making that information visible is the design goal. The study will tell us how useful it actually is.

Slide 6 · Currently skipped

Platform, Visuals & Human Factors

Three circles.

The design connects calendar cues, assignment information and work blocks, and settings for reminders and accessibility. Those pieces need to work together so a student can move from noticing a busy week to making a plan.
