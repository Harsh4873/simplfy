Slide 1 · Title

W(h)ither Spatial Hypertext?

If Avvai Chandrasekaran is here, don't read this intro. She leads. Atharv Chagi summarizes, and Ethan James Choe is his backup. You can add one question at the wrap-up. If you have to open, read the lines below. Don't walk through Aquanet, VIKI, and VKB. That belongs to the summary.

Howdy. I'm Harsh, and I'm the backup. This is Mark Anderson's paper, W(h)ither Spatial Hypertext, from HT '25.

Notes on a map can mean something from where they sit and how they look. You don't need an explicit link between them. He doesn't drop links. He gives them a smaller role, so the layout can carry meaning while the structure is still loose.

He's writing for readers whose idea of hypertext is the Web. The term was coined in 1993, the same year the Web opened to the public. He quotes Marshall and Shipman on "the ability to leave structure implicit and informal." That means you can leave the organization loose and unfinished.

There isn't one short definition, and he thinks that looseness has helped. A link can still exist even if you don't draw it.

The title is a pun. Whither means where is this going. Wither means fade. He writes that spatial hypertext deserves not to whither away.

I won't walk through Aquanet, VIKI, and VKB. That belongs to the summary.

Slide 2 · Setup

Where he says it stands

This is enough to ask from. Say both tool sentences. Don't collapse them. Page 107 names Tinderbox and Storyspace. Page 106 calls Tinderbox the only one for the general public.

This paper is his summary of the history, the tools, and where he thinks the work could go. It isn't a new user study.

Active research is declining, even though people are still interested. He asks if that's because we already know what spatial hypertext is, or because it needs re-contextualising, which means it needs a new setting.

He names two problems at once. There aren't enough tools, and there isn't a clear place to build the practice into work people already do.

He also asks if the result means anything to anyone but the person who made it. He calls that a beautiful mind palace of use only to its maker.

On the tools, he says two different things. Tinderbox and Storyspace, now on a common codebase, are the only two current public tools he names, and both are Mac only. He also calls Tinderbox the only spatial hypertext tool currently available to the general public.

Slide 3 · Question 1

Dormant, or just renamed?

If you only have time for one question, use this one. The question is not what year something shipped.

If we only have time for one question, I'd use this one.

Dormant means the research has gone quiet. He quotes another paper, Seven Hypertexts, which says spatial hypertext has become dormant.

He also says the people who started it never chose to define it too tightly, so other spatial work gets dismissed as not really spatial hypertext. Now that most tools are on the Web, the edge is harder to place.

He asks where all the spatial hypertexts are. Twine is his example of a Web tool that still uses a spatial layout to build a story. Tapestries, which he calls late beta, is essentially spatial hypertext in his reading, because what matters is how the pieces look and how they sit next to each other.

Google Scholar finds about 650 papers that mention spatial hypertext, and he says most of those mentions only glance at it.

The question isn't what year something shipped.

If spatial hypertext was never defined tightly, what would count as evidence that the work has gone quiet, rather than just showing up under another name?

Slide 4 · Question 2

Who makes the first arrangement?

A parser isn't required. He doesn't test the model idea here. Skip the VIKI paragraph unless you need the example.

Premature formalisation means committing to a structure before you understand the problem. Early link-typed systems made you decide the intent of a link, and what it pointed at, before that intent was fully formed.

A parser is a program that looks at the layout and tries to recognize structure, or to suggest a more formal version. He says a parser isn't required for something to count as spatial hypertext. The person makes the first arrangement. Arranging the map is already a kind of parsing, and the computer side can crowd that out.

He points at language models as one possible next step. He says those projects already use this with recommender systems and machine learning, and that large language models might talk to these tools and combine the views, the parsers, and the model. He doesn't test that here.

Example only if you need it. VIKI, then VKB, then Hof's iisys.

In VIKI the person laid the notes out, and the parser only suggested collections when they asked. It wasn't creating the layout. In VKB the person still controls the overall map. The Suggestion Manager surfaces structure. It doesn't really rearrange the map. Hof's system, iisys, has Mother, with three parsers: one for where the notes sit, one for how they look, and one for the order you interact with the notes.

If a model starts suggesting how the notes should be grouped, who is making sense of the material, and when is that suggestion too early?

Slide 5 · Questions 3 and 4

A private map, or a shared one

If Avvai already covered the mind palace, skip to question 4. Don't say nobody ever addressed a shareable form. Tinderbox gets part of the way there.

VKB is the last big system he covers. It listed four problems. The one he says wasn't really resolved is a publishable information space, meaning something you could hand to someone else as a finished piece. The map is flattened, even with nested pieces, and that's a weak fit for the linear narrative most publishing still wants. Tinderbox gets part of the way there.

He repeats Shipman's point that the maker and the user are often the same people, so ambiguity is easier for them. They already know the work. A general reader doesn't have that familiarity.

He asks what we can do with just the result, and whether it is a beautiful mind palace of use only to its maker. People making the case still have to say what problems this handles better than other processes, and in what context. Without the tools, that help stays hidden from a wider audience.

If the map mostly works for the person who built it, should spatial hypertext stay a private thinking tool?

The strength he names is exploring a problem and drawing out its shape. Our writeups and demos are still mostly a line. I'd ask the room to name one place in their own project where a loose structure would help, and one decision they wouldn't want left unstated.

Where would leaving the structure loose help a team, and where would it hide a decision everyone else needs to see?

If you have a few seconds: he quotes Kidd. Say the plain version. He wants both halves, the practice and the tools.

He also says the practice of using the environment is as valuable as any one tool, and that having the tools is what unlocks the practice. He wants both. He quotes Kidd to say the useful marks land on the person doing the work. Once the material has taught you something, you may not need to keep the source. Until it has, you also can't file it, because you don't know what it's for yet.
