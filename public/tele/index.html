Domain Experts, Design Novices

Howdy. This paper asks what happens when people who know a community well lead participatory design, even though they're new to design methods. Participatory design means people help shape the problem and create the solution.

Ko and her coauthors studied facilitators developing misinformation resources with their communities. Their main contribution is showing how professional experience and community relationships shape familiar design values, like trust and respect. Being new to design doesn't mean starting without useful expertise.

Slide 2

A year with four community teams

The study followed 15 facilitators across four teams from August 2022 to July 2023. They worked with rural libraries, rural K–12 educators, a community college, and an organization serving a local Black community.

The researchers used surveys and group interviews before the workshops and during debriefs. They also observed one session in each of three groups. They used reflexive thematic analysis, meaning they interpreted patterns across those accounts and notes.

These facilitators had substantial professional experience. Each team also had someone with PD experience or coursework and a flexible facilitation guide. That support matters when we interpret the findings.

Slide 3

Community knowledge shaped the design

The map shows the authors' interpretation: community and shared culture shaped the other four values. These values overlap. They aren't steps everyone has to follow in order.

First, shared culture helped people trust each other. Rural library facilitators drew on existing professional relationships. One quiet participant felt comfortable pointing out problems with an idea the group liked. Disagreement could be a sign of trust.

Second, participants helped change the work. Librarians corrected facilitators and brought quizzes back to their staff for feedback. That's co-production of knowledge: people help create and revise the result.

Third, the process and the result had to fit the community. The community organization placed PD within Disinformation Day, with food, vendors, and yoga. K–12 educators connected lessons to existing standards so other teachers could use them. These examples show respect for people's practices and a concern for solutions they can keep using.

Slide 4

Two strategies for novice facilitators

The authors suggest two strategies. First, be open to correction. The sequence here shows one facilitator's experience: he used the wrong term, a participant corrected him, and that correction shaped a later workshop. He acknowledged the mistake and let it change the work.

Second, allow the design process to look different across communities. Disinformation Day is their example of adapting participation to an existing community setting.

For HCI, this broadens who can lead design and who gets to define meaningful participation. Community knowledge belongs in decisions about the process itself. These are proposed strategies, not evidence that novices outperform professional designers.

Slide 5

Whose experience is missing?

The main limitation is that the analysis lacks the community participants' own accounts. The authors cite logistical challenges and wanting to protect participants' limited time. They observed some sessions, but they didn't observe the community organization's event.

That means we learn how facilitators understood their work, with limited evidence about how participants experienced their influence. My question is: what evidence would show that participants actually changed a design decision?

My takeaway is that community expertise can shape how PD works, while claims about equity still need the participants' perspective.
