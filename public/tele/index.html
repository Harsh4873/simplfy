Say this · about 1 min

Opening

The paper says participatory design isn't the workshop or the prototype, because the same workshop can happen in co-design and look exactly the same. What makes it participatory is why you run it and what people walk away with, they learn together and get a real say and the group can keep going after you leave, and the full version is laid out as an ideal that almost no real project actually pulls off.

While you walk around

Not the speech, so don't leave this open on the projector. The paper's side is under each question, and if a group has a real second answer then let it stand and ask which part of the paper they're using.

Quick words in case a group asks: the activity is the workshop or the study or the prototype, the mechanism is why that activity is supposed to matter (who gets a say, learning together, a group that stays), the output is the thing you can point at that day, and the outcome is what people got out of it after.

Question 1

Future Workshop

So if the team already picked the design before walking in then what's left for anyone to decide?

The paper's answer is comments but not the ending, because a Future Workshop runs critique where people say what's broken and then fantasy where they picture a future with no limits and then implementation where they build a plan that could really happen, and the shared vision only counts if the designers couldn't have guessed it since it's supposed to come from what each person brings in. The authors call that balancing power, and the mutual learning they want out of it depends on the designers actually handling who has power in the room, so if the design was already picked then the workshop can still happen but that mechanism didn't.

Say: "They can still comment but they can't change the result, so could anyone in that room have stopped the design?"

If they say people still learned something: learning with a fixed ending is thinner, though it's not nothing, so ask whether they'd still call that participatory design or just a meeting about a decision that was already made.

Question 2

After the researchers leave

So if a project ends and all that's left is the technology then what did it fail to build?

The paper's answer is the group that keeps it going, because fablab@school.dk put fabrication tools in schools but the team didn't just leave the lab, they worked with people from pupils up to politicians and tried to leave a group of teachers who'd keep the work in the schools. The authors call the loose bunch of people a knotwork and the version that lasts a network, and a lot of that work is unglamorous stuff like phone calls and hearings and meetings between the workshops, so if only the technology is left then that network is what they failed to build, since the machines can still be there while nobody's job is to keep them in use.

Say: "Who turns it on next year if the researchers are gone?"

If they defend the technology as the result: the authors care about the product too but they say most HCI stops there, and this project spent the extra time on the people who stay, so ask which one the paper treats as the thing that makes the work last.

Question 3

What counts as success

So when the prototype works but nobody in the room can name anything they personally got out of it then what should actually count as success?

The paper's answer is to count what people got and not just whether the prototype works, because a working prototype is an output (the thing you can point at) while Hertzum and Simonsen judge the project by whether real use matched what people asked for at the start, which is effects in actual use and not a usability test. The discussion also counts personal gains like a say in the project or a new skill and sometimes even a change in someone's later work or training, which they cite Bossen and coauthors for, so if the prototype works and nobody can name a gain then the product can be a success while the participatory design is still weak. And since the full picture is an ideal that almost nobody hits, a missing piece is incomplete and not an automatic fail, so the move is to name which piece you actually got.

Say: "The prototype works, so what did a person in the room gain that they can point at?"

If they only talk usability: Hertzum and Simonsen weren't judging how easy the system was, they were judging whether use matched the effect people asked for at the start.
