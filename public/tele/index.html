Slide 3 · 30 sec

Purpose

Students can already see when something is due. Canvas has the date. The calendar has the date. What they cannot see is how much effort that date actually takes.

Think about a Friday with a quiz and a lab report. On the calendar those are two bars of the same size. One might be half an hour. The other might be most of a weekend. The grid does not say which.

WAC is meant to make that load visible, so a student can spread the heavy work across the week instead of discovering it the night before.

Slide 4 · 35 sec

What it would do

The mission is to reduce that uncertainty, so the planning decision is based on the work, not only the date.

Show estimated effort next to the deadline. Help someone recognize when the week is actually heavy. Encourage them to start the larger assignment earlier, while there is still room. Support planning the whole week, not just surviving each due date one at a time.

The last line is the one we have not tested. Does putting that information on the calendar actually change how people plan? That is the study. It is not a result we have.

Slide 5 · 40 sec

Who it is for

Before we can begin designing the project that we have in mind, we need to think about who our main users would be.

The main user is a TAMU student who already keeps deadlines somewhere. Google Calendar, Canvas, or a list on paper. We are not designing for someone who has never planned a week.

What they need is to see how big the tasks actually are, so they stop stacking assignments with no information. What gets in the way is the due date itself. It hides the hours. And a bad guess does not stay on that one task. It penalizes the rest of that student's life: other classes, sleep, and anyone who planned around them.

We have not interviewed them. So the question on the slide is the next step. How do you want to see the work? Who it helps most is the student with a packed week, and the teammate stuck with that student's number.

Slide 6 · 30 sec

Plus signs in the title

Point. Don't narrate every date on the 21st through the 25th.

Same week, twice. In one picture the deadlines are just names, and the blue bars are the same length. In the other, the load is in the title: one plus, two pluses, or three. Class stays a small dot, 10am or 4pm, so color can still mean class.

You can read +++ without a legend. This is one design, not the decision. Color is not doing the work on this slide.

Slide 7 · 30 sec

The block grows, or it gets heavier

Different idea. Here the long task takes the afternoon. That milestone runs from morning to evening, and the block is tall. The shorter tasks are not. The size of the block is the size of the work.

The other picture keeps the clock the same, around 6 to 7, and makes the heavier task darker. We would be careful with that one. Color already means event type for a lot of people. If we reuse it for workload, we fight the legend they already have.

Slide 8 · 45 sec

Cognitive and social

Three problems, and they sit on top of each other.

Estimation bias. Students misjudge the hours. They look at a clean deadline instead of the real effort. I'll start Thursday. That first brief guess hardens. Once it is typed onto the calendar, it looks like an official estimate.

The group. One optimistic number anchors everyone else. If I say two hours, my teammates plan around two hours. The person who knew it was eight now looks slow, and their own week gets squeezed.

The screen. Exact hours on every event is a lot to read. Low, medium, and high is something you can see without studying the square. Color can make that clearer, and it can also confuse people, depending on what their calendar color already means.
